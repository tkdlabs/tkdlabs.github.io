A year has passed since I started my sabbatical. I took a 3-month break from work in July 2025 until October 2025, and then, a month later, I quit Google in November 2025. For me, this has been almost a year of "FIRE" lifestyle, where work is optional and self-driven, with no hard requirement to earn. I used it as an opportunity to stop and look at where I'm going and maybe find new directions.

FIRE itself was never the requirement, though; especially after this full year, I feel that coming back to work on a bigger project might be on the cards for me — not because I want money, but because I like camaraderie and working together with others on something cool. I want to start a reflective series about the last year, since I think I have some learnings that could be useful for folks planning something similar.

When I started the sabbatical, I was dealing with a few anxieties, on top of the work-related stress that triggered it in the first place — anxieties that a lot of people I chatted with from the FIRE community at Google faced too (most of them still before their own sabbatical):

# Am I gonna become a vegetable when I stop working?

I specifically remember this worry. Are the projects I dream about on weekends actually going to materialize? Or are they just "grass is greener" ideas that will evaporate as soon as I stop working, leaving me to decompose into a living vegetable, unable to do anything structured or plan-driven?

That's a common worry for people who work in effective organizations that structure the work for them. It's something I first ran into before the sabbatical, while working with a therapist. I realized that non-work life is extremely fuzzy — unstructured, blurry, and often lacking any mission or plan.

That's partly our own fault, granted: if you're a CEO or a director at a company, your whole role is to create structure for your employees to work within. It's actually very hard to organize, but even so, work life ends up fairly scoped and constrained. Life outside work is fuzzy by design, because it depends on other people, who are themselves inherently fuzzy, plus randomness and an almost unbounded number of areas of concern. From a leaking wall, to a random errand, to scheduled events like book clubs, meetups with friends, emergency calls, and so on — it's pretty much impossible to pack all of that into a coherent mission.

Often our non-working time is so limited that we can carve out only 10-20% of it for something structured and relevant to us; the rest goes to errands, obligations, and the random stuff that happens to everyone. To illustrate how stark the difference is: it's much easier to carve 15-20 hours out of a 40-hour work week for unexpected things — questions from teammates, emergencies, extra work — and still have 20-25 productive hours left. Apply that same ratio to a 16-hour weekend, and you're often left with just 2-4 hours of effective, productive time. The fuzziness of non-work time isn't something you can solve away.

So, coming back to this anxiety: it's fair to assume pretty much everyone faces it as soon as they consider stepping away from an exciting, absorbing, structured environment.

And admittedly, during my sabbatical, especially at the beginning, I was extremely focused on remaining productive. I remember giving a short talk at a Mustachians meetup in Longmont, about a week into my sabbatical, describing this exact anxiety about staying productive. People there told me that detaching from a work environment takes about a year. Now, a year on, I think that was a remarkably sharp observation.

Over the next month or two, I noticed something about the extra time you get for your own projects. Imagine swapping a 16-hour weekend for a 40-hour week, while still keeping a 16-hour weekend for socializing — that extra time has an amazing property: it gives you buffers for anything unexpected. Now the unexpected only eats up maybe 20-30% of that 40 hours, just like in a typical work week, and the rest is yours. A bad night's sleep or a late night (both rare now, almost nonexistent) affects me much less too, because of those buffers — I have the time and energy to recover, since I can make the next day a slower one. The whole recovery takes a day, instead of waiting for the weekend to catch up while still working. That, in turn, opens up more spontaneity and gives more satisfaction, since I can redirect my energy however the moment calls for.

Okay, enough for post #1 — post #2 will explore how I actually found structure, and whether I became a vegetable after all (I hope not :)
